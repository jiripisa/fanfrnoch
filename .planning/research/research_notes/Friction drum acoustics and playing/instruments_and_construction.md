## 1. Classification: what Hornbostel–Sachs (H–S) class 23 says, and how the world's friction drums fit into it

### Takeaway
H–S splits friction drums into **stick** drums (231: the stick goes *through* the membrane (231.1) or is *tied upright* to it (231.2)), **cord** drums (232: stationary (232.1) or *whirled* (232.2)) and **hand** friction drums (233). For a synth, the useful axes are what is rubbed (a rigid rod vs. a flexible tuft of horsehair or cord vs. the membrane itself), what it is rubbed with (a wet hand, a wet cloth, rosin), and the body (clay pot, staved barrel or tub, tin can, open-ended cylinder). The Czech fanfrnoch is a **cord/horsehair drum (232.11)**, not a stick drum.

### Cited Findings
- H–S 231 is defined as "Instruments in which the membrane is vibrated from a stick that is rubbed or used to rub the membrane." 231.1: the stick is inserted in a hole in the membrane. 231.11: the stick "can not be moved and is subject to rubbing, causing friction on the membrane" (examples given: cuíca, rommelpot). 231.12: the stick is semi-movable and is used to rub the membrane. 231.13: the stick is freely movable. 231.2: the stick is tied upright to the membrane. **[described] (full)** — [Wikipedia: List of membranophones by H–S number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_membranophones_by_Hornbostel%E2%80%93Sachs_number)
- H–S 232 "Friction drum with cord": "Instruments in which a cord, attached to the membrane, is rubbed". 232.1: the drum is held stationary while played (232.11: one usable membrane, e.g. buhay; 232.12: two usable membranes). 232.2: the drum is twirled on a cord that rubs in a notch of a stick the player holds (the whirled drum or Waldteufel type). 233: hand friction drums, where the membrane is rubbed by hand. **[described] (full)** — [same list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_membranophones_by_Hornbostel%E2%80%93Sachs_number). Note that this list puts the cuíca under both 231.11 and 232.11. That looks like a compiler's error: the cuíca has a bamboo stick (see section 4).
- General construction, per Wikipedia: a single membrane over a sound box (a pot, a jug or an open-ended hollow body). It is excited by friction from the fingers, through a cloth, or through a stick or cord fixed to the centre of the membrane. "The membrane may be depressed with the thumb to vary the pitch." In some drums the friction comes from spinning the drum around a pivot. **[described] (full)** — [Wikipedia: Friction drum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friction_drum). The article itself carries a no-inline-citations warning. Its general references are New Grove (1980) and Borg-Cardona, "The Maltese Friction Drum", *JAMIS* XXVII (2002), pp. 174–210.
- Britannica's definition: a membrane stretched over a vessel and sounded by rubbing "a stick or string passed through the membrane or tied upright from underneath". The instrument is found in Africa, Europe, Asia and the Americas, and its use is primarily ritual. **[described] (snippet; the full page returned 403)** — [Britannica: friction drum](https://britannica.com/print/article/220071)
- German lexicon definition of the Reibtrommel: a hollow body with a stretched membrane, set vibrating directly by hand or indirectly through a stick or string attached to the membrane. **[described] (snippet)** — [wissen.de: Reibtrommel](https://www.wissen.de/lexikon/reibtrommel). Universal-Lexikon adds that the body can be made of metal, clay or reed (Schilf). **[described] (snippet)** — [de-academic: Stabreibtrommel](https://de-academic.com/dic.nsf/dewiki/1319592)
- A Hartenberger collection page splits friction membranophones into drums "with sticks or cords" and "hand friction drums" (e.g. a tambourine roll). **[described] (full)** — [WMIC: Friction membranophones](https://wmic.net/africa/membranophones/friction-membranophones/)

### Inferences
- **[derived]** The H–S "fixed stick" and "horsehair/cord" classes differ mechanically in a way that matters for synthesis. A rod is **rigid**: it couples the stick-slip friction straight into the membrane centre and also radiates and resonates itself (reed or bamboo has its own longitudinal and bending modes). A horsehair tuft is a **flexible, tension-only** link, a bundle of many hairs each catching and slipping at its own moment. That should give a noisier, more "granular" excitation and a less pitched onset. The wording of the sources follows this split: rod drums are described as "brummend/humming", "frroep", a double bass; horsehair drums as "bellowing", "lowing of a bull", "growling" (see section 5).
- **[derived]** On the cuíca, an internal stick plus a free hand on the outside of the head allows deliberate pitch bends over an octave or more. Most European rod and horsehair drums have no such channel (the head is out of reach, or both hands are busy on the hair), so their pitch is largely fixed by the vessel, membrane tension and hair length, apart from the pressure and velocity of the stroke.

## 2. The Czech fanfrnoch (famfrnoch, bručák, brumbál, bukač, bukál): construction and playing

### Takeaway
Every Czech source found describes the fanfrnoch as a **horsehair friction drum**. A jug or pot of clay or tin (or a small barrel or wooden vessel) is covered with **bladder or leather**. A **bundle of horsehair (žíně), usually two bundles**, is threaded through the centre of the membrane and pulled alternately with **wet fingers** (water or saliva, vinegar in the past). The player usually holds it between the knees, or one person holds it while another plays. None of the sources found mentions a stick or rosin for the Czech instrument. None gives dimensions.

### Cited Findings
- Names given: fanfrnoch, famfrnoch, bukač, brumbál, bukál. It is an old Czech folk rhythmic instrument from **Chodsko** (the Chod region), a membranophone of indefinite pitch. **[described] (full)** — [cs.wikipedia: Fanfrnoch](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanfrnoch)
- Construction per cs.wikipedia: the body is a vessel (*džbán*, a jug) of **clay or tin (hliněná nebo plechová nádoba)**. A **membrane of animal bladder or skin (měchýř / kůže)** is stretched over the mouth. Through the middle of the membrane a **bundle of horsehair (svazek žíní), usually two**, is threaded. **[described] (full)** — [cs.wikipedia: Fanfrnoch](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanfrnoch)
- Playing per cs.wikipedia: the player wets the fingers and pulls them along the horsehair, and friction makes the sound. The instrument is usually held between the knees and played **alternately with the left and right hand**. Two-person play is also known (one person holds the vessel, the other plays), which is practical when walking. Traditionally only boys and men played it. It accompanied carolling at New Year and Christmas, and the article notes the comic character of the performance. **[described] (full)** — [cs.wikipedia: Fanfrnoch](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanfrnoch)
- Pitch per cs.wikipedia: a larger vessel gives a **deeper, stronger tone** and a smaller one a **higher tone**. No measurements are given. **[claimed] (full)** — [cs.wikipedia: Fanfrnoch](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanfrnoch). The article cites F. V. Vykoukal, *Rok v starodávných slavnostech našeho lidu* (1901), pp. 7 and 81; A. Modr, *Hudební nástroje* (1981); L. Vrkočová, *Slovníček základních hudebních pojmů* (2005); J. Š. Baar & R. Lužík, *Chodské písně a pohádky* (1976), pp. 545–546; V. Čech, *Květy* 35 (1895), p. 125.
- A maker and educator blog: "Na tělo fanfrnochu se dá použít starý džbán, hrnec nebo třeba soudek." ("For the body of a fanfrnoch you can use an old jug, a pot, or even a small barrel.") "Hrdlo džbánku se překryje koženou blankou … uprostřed ní se vytvoří dírka, kterou se provléknou svazky žíní." ("The neck of the jug is covered with a leather membrane … a small hole is made in its middle, and bundles of horsehair are threaded through it." The page actually spells it "žní", evidently a typo for žíní.) "Na fanfrnoch se hraje střídavě oběma rukama, které si hráč musí předem namočit do vody nebo naslinit." ("The fanfrnoch is played alternately with both hands, which the player must first dip in water or wet with saliva.") The page also says vinegar was used in the past for a better sound, and that the instrument can be tuned by **changing the length of the horsehair or the tension of the membrane**. It calls it "only a rhythmic, accompanying instrument" that cannot play melodies. **[described] (full)** — [Nesladkář: Vyrob si fanfrnoch!](https://nesladkar.cz/blog-post/vyrob-si-fanfrnoch/)
- South Bohemian Museum event notice (maker Miroslav Stecher): "Vyrábí se buď ze džbánu nebo plechové či dřevěné nádoby, která se potáhne blánou podobně jako bubínek. Do kůže se ještě protáhnou koňské žíně, za které se potom tahá a tím fanfrnoch vydává zvuk." ("It is made either from a jug or from a tin or wooden vessel, which is covered with a membrane much like a small drum. Horsehair is threaded through the skin, and pulling on it makes the fanfrnoch sound.") "Nástroj se drží většinou mezi koleny a hraje se střídavě levou a pravou rukou." ("The instrument is mostly held between the knees and played alternately with the left and right hand.") **[described] (full)** — [JCTED / Jihočeské muzeum: Víte, co je fanfrnoch?](https://www.jcted.cz/vite-co-je-fanfrnoch-ve-ctvrtek-muzete-videt-jak-se-vyrabi/?liveMode=1)
- Further Czech dictionary and encyclopaedic entries (Hudební scéna, Wiktionary, cs.wikipedia "Brumbál") agree: a jug of clay or tin, a membrane of bladder or leather, a bundle of horsehair through the centre, pulled with wet fingers. They place it in Bohemia, Moravia and Silesia for New Year carolling. "Brumbál" is an old form of *brundibál* (bumblebee, grouch), an onomatopoeia for a low buzz. **[described] (snippet)** — [Hudební scéna: Famfrnoch, fanfrnoch](https://www.hudebni-scena.cz/slovnik-hudebnich-pojmu/famfrnoch-fanfrnoch-99); [cs.wiktionary: fanfrnoch](https://cs.wiktionary.org/wiki/fanfrnoch); [cs.wikipedia: Brumbál](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brumb%C3%A1l)
- iFolklor has an audio podcast item, "Novoroční koleda s fanfrnochem" (a New Year carol with fanfrnoch): a possible reference recording. **[described] (snippet; I did not listen to it)** — [iFolklorní zpěvník](https://www.ifolklor.cz/audio-podcasty/ifolklorni-zpevnik/novorocni-koleda-s-fanfrnochem/)

### Inferences
- **[derived]** In synth terms, the Czech instrument is a **two-strand horsehair drum played alternately**. Each hand stroke is a separate, short excitation burst. With two hands alternating, this produces a natural "grunt–grunt" rhythmic pulse. That fits the synth's rhythm-first design: one pad = one stroke, with the attack set by hand speed and wetness.
- **[derived]** The tuning levers the sources name (vessel size, hair length, membrane tension) map onto the knobs *body size*, *excitation-string length/compliance* and *head tension*.
- **[derived]** "Wet fingers vs. vinegar" is a friction-coefficient control: more stick or more slip. Rosin is not attested for the Czech instrument in the sources I found. It is attested for the Polish burczybas (a rosined glove), the Dutch rommelpot (a resined stick) and the orchestral lion's roar.

### Gaps
- **I did not find Karel Kurfürst's (or Pavel Kurfürst's) organology text, Národopisný ústav material, or any eSbírky record of a fanfrnoch with dimensions.** A search of esbirky.cz returned only irrelevant results. The Czech "bručák" name was not confirmed as a separate instrument: in the sources it appears only as an alternative name. Museum records with measured diameters and volumes (e.g. from Národní muzeum – České muzeum hudby, the Chodsko museum in Domažlice, or the Jihočeské muzeum) remain an open lead.
- No Czech source describes the fanfrnoch's sound beyond "deeper, stronger" vs. "higher". There are no Hz values and no onomatopoeic descriptions beyond the name itself (brumbál = buzzing/droning).

## 3. Central and Eastern European relatives: German, Dutch/Flemish, Polish, Hungarian, Romanian, Ukrainian

### Takeaway
The region splits roughly into a **western and northern stick zone** (German Brummtopf/Rummelpott, Dutch and Flemish rommelpot/foekepot, Danish rumlepot, Austrian Büllhäfen: a clay pot, pig bladder and a reed or stick, sometimes resined) and an **eastern horsehair zone** (Czech fanfrnoch, Polish/Kashubian burczybas, Romanian buhai, Ukrainian buhay, Moldavian Csángó "bika"). **Hungary has both** (köcsögduda with a reed or a horsehair tuft). The eastern horsehair drums are larger (staved barrels, about 30–44 cm), are often bottomless, and are described as **bull-like bellowing**.

### Cited Findings
**Germany / Austria (Brummtopf, Rummelpott, Rommelpott, Fukkepott, Büllhäfen, Waldteufel)**
- de.wikipedia: a friction drum with one head (*einfellige Trommel*) on a pot, with an "abgebundenen Stelle in der Fellmitte" (a tied-off spot in the centre of the skin) into which a **notched stick** is inserted. The skin is tied to the notch with string. Pots are of **clay or wood, some partly filled with water**. Rubbing the stick by hand or with a (possibly damp) cloth makes a "brummendes Geräusch" (a humming or droning noise), and the pot amplifies it. Different pot sizes are used for different pitches. Names: Brummtopf, Rummelpott, Rommelpott, Fukkepott (DE); Waldteufel (Prussia, Berlin); Büllhäfen (Austria). **[described] (full)** — [de.wikipedia: Brummtopf](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brummtopf). It cites F. J. de Hen, "Rommelpot", *Grove Dictionary of Musical Instruments* vol. 4 (2014), p. 334, and G. Haid, "Büllhäfen", *Oesterreichisches Musiklexikon*.
- A music retailer describes the same notched-stick construction, with the skin bound into the notch by a string winding. Pitch is said to depend on drum diameter and head tension. **[claimed] (snippet)** — [Musikhaus Schlaile: Waldteufel](https://www.schlaile.de/br.php/Waldteufel.8072.html); [klangkling.at](http://klangkling.at/effektinstrument-reibtrommel-waldteufel/)
- The name "Waldteufel" is used for **two different things**: a Prussian/Berlin name for the stick Brummtopf, and a **whirled cord drum** (232.2). **Netherlands / Flanders (rommelpot, foekepot, koenckelpot)**
- nl.wikipedia: "een aardewerken pot" (an earthenware pot) with "een varkensblaas erover gespannen" (a pig's bladder stretched over it). ### Inferences
- **[derived]** The eastern horsehair drums are physically **big**: the Horniman buhai is 315 × 287 mm, the Kashubian burczybas 435 × 390 mm, the Moldavian buhai 41 × 30.5 cm. They are **often bottomless** (open at one end). An open-ended tube has no sealed air spring behind the membrane, so it behaves acoustically differently from a closed pot (the rommelpot or Czech jug): it has tube resonances, and the membrane is not stiffened by trapped air. This supports two "body" models in the synth: **closed vessel** (pot, jug, tin) and **open tube/barrel** (buhai, burczybas, cuíca).
- **[derived]** Horsehair lengths of 0.5–0.7 m (Horniman: 670 mm; Hungarian "a few spans") mean the player draws long strokes. That suggests sustained grunts lasting roughly as long as an arm-length pull, chopped into a rhythm by alternating hands. The stick drums are played with short up-down strokes on a 1–2 span reed.
- **[derived]** The Czech fanfrnoch, built on a jug or pot (a closed vessel), sits between the two zones: the eastern excitation (horsehair) on the western body (a closed clay or tin pot). That may be why Czech sources describe it as smaller and higher-pitched than the bull-roaring Romanian and Kashubian barrels. This is an inference only; no source compares them directly.

### Gaps
- No Czech, Slovak or Polish source gives the Hz or note of any specimen. No Slovak-specific material was found: Slovak names and instruments remain unresearched, and I found no evidence for or against a distinct Slovak tradition.
- The Romanian ethnographic article by V. Tătulea, "Instrumente muzicale tradiționale în Oltenia" (*Oltenia. Studii și comunicări – Etnografie* IX, 1999), likely has construction details, but the PDF returned 403: [biblioteca-digitala.ro](https://biblioteca-digitala.ro/reviste/Oltenia/09-Oltenia-studii-si-comunicari-etnografie-IX-1999_033.pdf).
- I did not check the MIM Brussels rommelpot holdings or Grove's "Rommelpot" entry (de Hen 2014) directly.

## 4. Southern Europe and Latin America: zambomba, sarronca, putipù/caccavella, cuíca (and relatives)

### Takeaway
The Iberian and Italian drums are **external fixed-reed drums** on clay jugs, tubs or tins, with a skin or bladder head. They are rubbed with a wet hand and described as low and hoarse ("like a double bass"). The Brazilian **cuíca** is the outlier: an **internal bamboo stick** in an **open-ended metal cylinder**, typically **8–10 in (20–25 cm) in diameter and about 26–30 cm long**, rubbed with a wet cloth while a thumb or finger on the outside of the head bends the pitch **over an octave or more**. Its sound is described as squeaky, laughing, "high-pitched" and vocal. It is the only friction drum in this survey that is played melodically.

### Cited Findings
**Spain (zambomba)**
- The Centro de Documentación Musical de Andalucía catalogue describes the Andalusian zambomba as a clay friction drum with a skin or bladder head and a cane (*carrizo*) fixed at the centre. The sound is "grave, ronco y de altura indeterminada" (low, hoarse and of indeterminate pitch). Example: a **zambomba-cántaro from Hinojosa del Duque (Córdoba)**, an earthenware jug about **44.5 cm high with a mouth of about 9 cm**, thrown and left as biscuit (unglazed), with a **pig-bladder** head; the cane is rubbed with the fingers **[measured/described] (snippet only; the full page failed with a TLS certificate error)**. The Guadix (Granada) *orzas* use **goat or kid skin**, and the skin is rubbed with garlic to tension it. Used at Christmas and at the pig slaughter (*matanza*), and also for *rondas*. **(snippet)** — [CDMA: Zambomba – Cántaro](https://www.centrodedocumentacionmusicaldeandalucia.es/membranofonos/membranofonos-de-friccion/zambomba-cantaro); [CDMA item 26](https://www.centrodedocumentacionmusicaldeandalucia.es/-/26-zambomba)
- Wikipedia (en): zambomba is played with a rod or rope and associated with Christmas carols. **Portugal (sarronca, ronca, zamburra)**

The skin is goat, sheep or rabbit (coarse canvas when skins were scarce). **Afro-Cuban**
- The Kinfuíti, a related wooden-bodied drum, is played with a stick inside the drum and produces a low sound. **[described] (snippet)** — [Michel Conci blog: Rub-A-Drum](http://michelconci.blogspot.com/2016/09/rub-drum-brazil-cuica-and-reggae.html)

### Inferences
- **[derived]** The cuíca's control channel (a free hand pressing the head) lets pitch be set per note. That maps naturally to the synth's **keyboard mode** (MIDI note → head tension or pitch), with glides between notes as the cuíca's characteristic "laugh/whoop" contour. The European drums map better to **fixed-pitch pads** with little or no pitch bend within a stroke.
- **[derived]** The "metallic" spectrum of steel-shell cuícas versus the clay or wood bodies of the folk drums suggests a body/shell-material knob (clay/wood = damped, few body modes; thin metal = ringing, inharmonic high partials).

### Gaps
- **No measured fundamental frequencies or note ranges for the cuíca were found.** "An octave or more" (NIME) is the only quantitative range statement. The Hypercuíca paper's mapping table (pitch shift ±24 semitones, EQ 10–965 Hz) describes its effects processing, not the acoustic instrument.
- I did not find Contemporânea or Gope official spec sheets with depth and weight. Only a Gope shop listing title was seen: "CUICA … 30CMX10" 10 AFIN ARO LARGO", i.e. **30 cm × 10 in, 10 tuning lugs, wide hoop** **[measured] (snippet)** — [Gope loja](https://loja.gope.net/cuica/10/cuica-alum-30cmx10-10-afin-aro-largo-preto-e-preto-bal3010cpp)
- The CDMA zambomba measurements are snippet-only and should be confirmed by opening the page in a browser.

## 5. African and Asian friction drums, and the whirled and string drums

### Takeaway
African friction drums are concentrated in **Central and Southern Africa** (Angola, southern DR Congo, Zambia, Botswana, South Africa). They are dominantly **internal-stick** types (a stick on the underside of the head inside an open cylinder or pot, rubbed with a wet hand or stick), and they are **very low-pitched** and associated with leopards and lions and with ancestral voices. In Asia the documented examples I found are mostly **small whirled toy drums** (the Indian giligilipī, 4.4 cm across, "squeaky"). The orchestral **lion's roar / string drum** is a rosined-cord member of the cord class.

### Cited Findings
- Congo, *puita*: "a friction drum of the Bambala and Bahuangana of the Congo, with cylindrical body, laced membrane, and friction stick of palm rib". **[described] (snippet)** — [RILM Music Encyclopedias: Puita](https://rme.rilm.org/articles/mcd06070/1.0/mcd06070)
- Zulu, *ingungu*: a stick set inside a clay pot covered with goatskin, used by girls in puberty rites (*ukuthomba*) to accompany songs. Another source describes goatskin laced over a hollow gourd, rubbed with a wet stick, giving a "low-pitched, humming sound". **[described] (snippet)** — [Oxford Reference: Ingungu](https://www.oxfordreference.com/display/10.1093/acref/9780199743391.001.0001/acref-9780199743391-e-3395); [Unarchived Living Cultures: Ingungu](https://unarchivedlivingcultures.org/portfolios/ingungu/)
- Distribution: friction drums in Africa are found mainly in Angola, southern DR Congo, parts of Zambia and Botswana. **[described] (snippet)** — [Britannica: African music – Membranophones](https://www.britannica.com/art/African-music/Membranophones)
- Central African internal-stick drums are "extremely low-pitched", linked to the voice of big cats and of the dead, and often "heard but cannot be seen by ordinary people" in ritual. **[described] (full)** — [Hypercuíca, NIME 2026](https://nime.org/proceedings/2026/nime2026_81.pdf)
- The Hartenberger collection holds Lwena and Tikar friction drums. No details were shown on the overview page. **(full)** — [WMIC](https://wmic.net/africa/membranophones/friction-membranophones/)
- India, *giligilipī* (Koizumi collection, Tokyo University of the Arts, no. 198): a clay bowl body with a **paper membrane**, **depth 3.0 cm, diameter 4.4 cm** **[measured]**. - Lion's roar (orchestral string drum): a drumhead with a cord or horsehair through it. The cord is **rosined and rubbed with coarse fabric or a glove**, and the sound resembles a lion's roar. Varèse scored it in *Amériques*. **[described] (snippet)** — [Wikipedia: Lion's roar (instrument)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lion%27s_roar_(instrument)); [LA Phil: Amériques](https://www.laphil.com/works/ameriques); [Berliner Philharmoniker](https://www.berliner-philharmoniker.de/en/titelgeschichten/20182019/temple-bells-lion-roars-and-sirens/)

### Inferences
- **[derived]** Size and pitch line up across the survey. Whirled toys (4–6 cm, paper or bladder) are described as **squeaky** or creaking. Pots and jugs (about 20–45 cm) are humming or hoarse. Big barrels and the African internal-stick drums are **bellowing or roaring**. The orchestral lion's roar (a large drum with a rosined cord) is a bridge to the "roar" end. That suggests the synth's size/register macro could run from cicada-squeak through rommelpot hum to buhai bellow.

### Gaps
- No Japanese, Chinese, Indonesian or other East Asian friction drums were found in this pass. That is a gap in my search, not evidence that none exist.

### Inferences
- **[derived]** Why a smaller closed vessel sounds higher: the air sealed behind the head acts as an extra spring on the membrane. The spring constant scales roughly with (head area)² / (cavity volume), so a small jug stiffens the head more and raises its frequency. For open-ended barrels and tubes (buhai, burczybas, cuíca) this cavity stiffening is largely absent, and pitch is set by membrane tension and mass plus open-tube resonances. This is a standard membrane–cavity model, not taken from a friction-drum source.
- **[derived]** "Indefinite pitch" in the folk literature probably reflects (a) inharmonic membrane modes, (b) noisy, irregular stick-slip from horsehair or a wet hand, and (c) pitch that drifts with stroke pressure. It does not mean the sound has no pitch centre: the cuíca shows the same mechanism can be clearly pitched when the head is controlled.
- **[derived]** As a rough register guide from the descriptions only: whirled toys squeak (high), the cuíca spans mid to high with whoops, the stick pots hum or "double-bass" (low to mid), and the horsehair barrels and African internal-stick drums bellow or roar (low). No source gives Hz values to anchor this.

### Gaps
- **No Hz or note-name data for any traditional friction drum** was found, including for the cuíca, which has the most technical literature. Spectral analysis of recordings (e.g. the iFolklor fanfrnoch carol, Horniman/Europeana audio, commercial cuíca sample libraries) would be needed. That is outside this text-research pass.
- Vessel volumes are given nowhere. Membrane diameters for clay-pot instruments are given only for the Hinojosa zambomba (a mouth of about 9 cm, snippet).

## 7. How sources describe the sound (museum and ethnographic wording)

### Takeaway
The vocabulary clusters by type. Horsehair barrels **bellow or low like a bull** (buhai, köcsögduda "bika", burczybas "growl"). The cuíca **squeaks, laughs and whoops**. Whirled toys **squeak or creak**.

## 8. Modern commercial instruments and their specifications

### Takeaway
The only friction drum sold at volume is the **cuíca**: brass, chrome-plated or stainless-steel and aluminium shells, **6–12 in diameter (8–10 in typical), about 26–30 cm long, 8–10 tension rods**, goatskin or synthetic head, bamboo stick. European friction drums are sold mainly as craft or effect instruments (Waldteufel whirled drums with goat or buffalo skin, lion's roar on stands); I found no detailed specs for them.

### Inferences
- **[derived]** Cuíca bodies are long, open-ended thin-metal tubes (about 26–30 cm) with heads of 20–25 cm. A cuíca preset should therefore model an open tube with a metallic shell; the European folk drums need closed clay or wood vessels, or wide open wooden barrels.

### Gaps
- No official Contemporânea, LP or Gope spec sheet was read in full. Head thickness, stick dimensions (length and diameter of the gambito) and weights were not found.
- No commercial fanfrnoch, buhai or rommelpot with published specs was found. Czech makers such as Miroslav Stecher make them as crafts.
